Spend rose, royalty did not. Why, and what to do about it

2026-08-13. Ads data complete and settled through 2026-08-11. Writes have been frozen since 2026-08-07, so everything below is a proposal and nothing has been applied.

Seven analysts worked this independently against the warehouse, and every claim strong enough to change a decision was then handed to a separate reviewer whose only job was to destroy it. 38 claims went to adversarial review and 3 survived intact. The ones that died are listed near the bottom, because what did not hold is as useful as what did.

The answer, in six lines

  • The account spent $9.74/day over the last settled week and kept $4.15/day of KDP-read royalty. Net -$5.59/day.
  • $4.31/day of that buys nothing at all, and cutting it costs zero of the 12 orders that week.
  • Spend rose because of volume, not price. Cost per click actually fell, $0.342 to $0.296.
  • Royalty did not follow because the added volume was not the same kind of volume. It went to nine marketplaces that have never produced an order and to an ad type that cannot earn a click.
  • The lane that does work, US auto-search, is 10% of the loss and 17 of the 25 orders. Broad-match keywords and the foreign expansion are 74% of it.
  • We are not stuck on seven keywords. 16 targets across 9 books have converted, discovery is accelerating (1, 1, 2, 7, 9 new converting queries per week), and a new one costs $2.62 to find.
  • The exploring half of the account is the only part that makes money. The 17 small US auto campaigns on non-flagship books return +$5.72 on $12.52 of spend. The four big flagship auto campaigns return -$15.49 on $39.91.

The US leg of that wave was never really a test. 47,365 of its 49,498 impressions, 95.7%, went to the ASIN targeting lanes, and its search lanes took 6 clicks on 2,133 impressions for $1.73. The proposals

1. Pause every non-US campaign. Saves $3.52/day. Costs 0 orders. 116 clicks and $28.17 across nine markets, zero orders ever. Reviewers were right that no single market has enough clicks to be killed on its own numbers - UK is the closest at 34 clicks - so this is a decision about the foreign programme as a whole, which is where the evidence lives, and not about Italy or Spain individually. Section 5 has the mechanism.

2. Pause the 46 ASIN targeting groups still enabled. Saves about $0.15/day. Costs 0 orders. Lifetime these two auto groups have taken 62,826 impressions for 27 clicks and zero orders, a 0.043% click-through rate against 0.832% on auto-search. They fail the click test long before the conversion test ever applies.

The saving here is smaller than a seven-day average suggests, and it is worth saying why: the 2026-08-08 pause is still propagating, so this lane averaged $0.29/day across the week but spent $0.15 on the most recent settled day. Most of this cut has already happened. The 46 that remain are mostly foreign and go with proposal 1 anyway.

3. Pause broad and exact match keywords. Saves $0.49/day. Costs 0 orders at the current run rate. Broad match is the single most expensive mistake in the account's history: $50.47 spent, 4 orders, $8.76 of royalty, net -$41.71. Its cost per click is $0.639 against $0.340 on auto-search, and it converts at 5.1% against 11.0%. It buys worse traffic at twice the price. Most of the damage is already done and most of those keywords are already paused; this closes the rest.

4. Keep phrase match. $0.72/day for 2 orders and $0.56/day of royalty. It is close to break-even and it is a proven lane. Cutting it would save $0.16/day and give up a working discovery route.

Total: $4.31/day, $129/month, for zero orders given up.

And that is the ceiling of cutting. Deleting everything the data condemns still leaves the account losing about a dollar a day. No further cut reaches profit, because what remains is already the best thing in the account. From here it is price and product, which is section 7.

What this does not do

It does not make the account profitable. US auto-search still runs at -$1.12/day because the flagship's royalty is $2.19 against a $2.51 cost per order. That gap closes with price, not with bids. Section 7.

3. Keeping discovery on

Abhay's worry was being stuck with seven keywords. The premise is out of date.

16 distinct targets across 9 distinct ASINs have converted, not seven. The old count collapsed eleven separate auto targets, on eleven different books in eleven different campaigns, into one text label.

Discovery is accelerating, not stalling. New converting queries found per week:

week ofnew converting queries
2026-07-061

US auto-search has spent $52.43 lifetime and found 20 distinct converting queries: $2.62 each, 7.7 clicks each, about 925 impressions each.

Why harvesting them keeps failing, and what to do instead

Every one of the 20 converting queries converted exactly once, and 18 of the 20 have never appeared again on any day. There is no such thing as a proven search term on this account under the two-orders rule. That is why turning them into exact-match keywords does nothing: on 2026-08-08 five of them were lifted from $0.18-$0.24 to $0.40, above the delivery floor, and in the days since they have taken one impression between them.

Keyword research fares no better. 2,343 mined candidate phrases and 43 reverse-ASIN phrases have zero overlap with the 20 queries that actually converted.

The mechanism is that these are 3 to 8 word long-tail queries that occur once. Auto targeting finds them because it is matching intent, not strings. A keyword list cannot enumerate them in advance and a bid raise cannot summon them.

So the discovery engine is auto loose-match on US books, and the right move is to feed it rather than to harvest from it. Concretely:

  • Keep US auto-search funded at its current $4.71/day. It is the only lane that both earns clicks and converts.
  • Stop harvesting to exact match. It has been tried three times and produced one impression. decisions.json -> launch_beats_raise already says to launch a fresh auto campaign instead of raising a bid on a dark one.
  • Explore/exploit: roughly 45/55 by spend, and it is already close to that. The flagship took $20.20 and the rest of the catalogue $21.01 in the loose lane over Aug 1-11, for 8 orders each. There is no case for concentrating harder on the flagship: it has the account's lowest royalty at $2.19, and the rest of the catalogue returns $2.89 per order. Hold the split.
  • Stopping rule: review a book's auto exposure when it reaches 30 clicks, not before. Today 61 of 71 US books in the auto lanes are under 10 clicks, which means nothing in the catalogue is judgeable and nothing should be cut for failing to convert.

3b. The finding that inverts the strategy

Split the US auto-search lane by campaign size. Small campaigns are the exploratory ones on non-flagship books; the big ones are the flagship.

campaignsclicksspendordersroyaltynet
the flagship's auto campaigns4115$39.9111$24.42-$15.49
the small exploring tail1739$12.526$18.24+$5.72

The exploring tail is the only profitable slice in the entire account. It returns $0.468 of royalty per click against a $0.321 cost per click. The flagship returns $0.212 against $0.347.

This matters because the instinct on an account like this is always to concentrate on the proven book, and the proven book is the biggest loser in it.

Be precise about why, because half of this is luck and half is not. The tail converts at 15.4% against the flagship's 9.6%, and that difference is not significant (p=0.32) - it will regress. What will not regress is the royalty per order: $3.04 against $2.22. Those are KDP-read royalties on books that are simply priced higher, and no amount of further data changes them.

So the durable version of the claim is: at equal conversion rates the rest of the catalogue returns 37% more royalty per order than the flagship. The measured +$5.72 overstates the edge; the direction of it is structural.

The practical consequence is that "keep discovery on" is not a concession made for learning. It is the best-returning line item in the account, and the explore/exploit split should not move toward the flagship.

Two structural notes on where not to put it.

Budget is not a constraint anywhere. Enabled campaigns offer $674.49/day of budget and the account spends 1.5% of it. Raising a budget is not a lever, and three converting campaigns hitting their cap costs the account almost nothing.

Bids above the floor are not a lever either. Sub-floor bids have taken 127,835 impressions, 76% of every impression this account has ever served, at 0.128% click-through against 0.620% above the floor, and produced 2 of 25 orders. The floor separates useful inventory from junk. It does not separate delivery from silence, and the old wording of that rule ("nothing wins an impression below $0.35") was refutable in a single query.

5. RCA: why the international ads return nothing

116 clicks, $28.17, zero orders, across nine marketplaces. Five candidate causes were tested. Three are dead.

REFUTED - distribution. All 91 books are is_distributed with a KDP-read royalty in every advertised marketplace. No undistributed book took a single foreign impression.

REFUTED - pricing. Foreign list prices are properly localised. Zero of 91 German prices exceed their US twin.

REFUTED - the English-language explanation. Non-English stores actually out-clicked the English ones, 0.171% against 0.145%, and the difference is not significant. The UK is an English store selling English books and runs 0.140%, the second-worst rate in the account.

SUPPORTED, and it is the mechanism - we bid below the delivery floor abroad, so we buy the inventory nobody competes for. Median foreign bid is $0.24 against $0.35 in the US. Only 22,168 of 71,983 foreign impressions were bought at or above the floor; 69% was detail-page placement. And we bid low abroad because that is what the royalty justifies: at 8.08% conversion, only 9 books in the UK, 2 in Canada and exactly 1 each in Germany, France, Italy, Spain and the Netherlands clear the royalty a floor bid needs. Australia and Mexico: none.

SUPPORTED - the same book earns a quarter of the clicks abroad. This is the cleanest test available, because it holds the product constant:

the books that have converted somewhereimpressionsclicksCTR
in the US30,6712400.782%
in the other nine markets13,202300.227%

Same books, same auto lanes, a 3.4x gap on 13,202 foreign impressions. Whatever is wrong abroad is not the books.

A localisation problem does exist, but it is narrower than "English books on foreign stores". In Italy and France the auto lanes are buying local-language searches for an English-language product: 76% of sampled Italian click spend came from Italian queries like *libri sullo spazio eta 6/7 anni*, exactly the right buyer intent in the wrong language. That is a relevance failure inside two specific markets, not a general one.

And the spend is inversely allocated to demonstrated demand. Set foreign ad spend against what each marketplace has ever actually paid us, across all channels:

Italy and Spain have never earned a cent in the account's history and took 36% of the foreign budget. Canada has earned $28.55 organically and took 0.5%.

Verdict: pause the foreign programme. Not because any one country is proven dead - none is - but because the programme as a whole has spent past the point where a zero is bad luck, and because the mechanism that would have to be fixed (bid at the floor) is blocked by the royalty in those markets. If it is ever reopened, reopen it as one properly funded test in Canada or the UK on the highest-royalty book at a true floor bid, not as 484 campaigns across nine markets at $0.24.

The largest gap in this analysis is here. book_days, review_events, serp_days, category_days and book_listing_versions are 100% US. We have no observational data on any foreign shelf: no rating, no review count, no rank, no competitor set. The single most likely remaining explanation - that our books carry no social proof in those stores - is currently unmeasurable, and it would take a scraper pass per marketplace to answer.

6. RCA: why the other books' auto ads return nothing

They do not return nothing. That framing is wrong, and correcting it changes the strategy.

Non-flagship books convert perfectly normally once they get a click. In the US loose-match lane over Aug 1-11:

impressionsclicksCTRordersconversion
flagship2,089582.776%813.8%
every other book8,529630.739%812.7%

Equal orders on roughly equal spend, and the conversion rates are statistically indistinguishable. The handicap is entirely click-through: they cannot earn a click, not convert one. Since Amazon charges per click and impressions are effectively free, that costs time rather than money.

What predicts click-through is not what everyone assumed.

  • Reviews: does not survive a control. The correlation is real at r=+0.82 but removing the flagship alone drops it to +0.41, and removing the three "My First Book on..." titles inverts it - books with reviews 0.504%, books with none 0.564%. And reviews cannot be the lever anyway: the whole 86-book catalogue gained 8 reviews in 15 days, while a top-10 slot in its category runs on a median of 3,479 ratings.
  • Price: no effect. Nine coloring books published together, all with zero reviews, at three prices: $9.99 gives 0.124%, $11.99 gives 0.357%, $12.99 gives 0.264%. The sign is wrong for a "too expensive" story.
  • Series: this is the real one. In the loose lane the three "My First Book on..." toddler reference titles run 1.969% against 0.471% for every other print book, a 4.2x gap. Excluding the flagship entirely and using only Jungle (1 review) and Ocean (4 reviews), it is still 1.296% against 0.471%. Two low-review books beat the entire rest of the catalogue.

The readable conclusion is that format and category intent earn the click. A toddler reference book about a concrete subject that parents search for by name gets clicked; a narrative picture book with an invented character does not, whatever it costs and whatever its reviews say.

7. The thing none of this fixes

Even after every cut above, US auto-search runs at -$1.12/day, because the flagship's cost per order is $2.51 against $2.19 of royalty.

A correction to what this section said when first published. It claimed the $12.99 read landing 2026-08-14 does not reach break-even and that $14.99 was the first price that does. That used the flagship's blended cost per click of $0.495, which is inflated by the broad-match clicks the plan above deletes. On the lane that actually survives the cuts, the arithmetic is different and much better:

value
flagship US auto lane77 clicks, 10 orders, $26.84
its own cost per click$0.3486
its own conversion rate12.99%
royalty needed to break even$2.68
royalty today, at $11.89$2.19

So $12.99 clears it, on the lane we are keeping. Estimates of the required royalty across the analysis ran $2.68 to $2.96 depending on the window and the conversion rate used, and $2.85 sits inside that band rather than comfortably above it. Read tomorrow's result as "roughly break-even, and finally on the right side of the line", not as a solved problem.

The cuts and the price test are complements, not alternatives. $12.99 is only "not enough" if we keep paying $0.639 a click for broad match.

Two product levers exist and neither is an ad decision:

Trim size, worth about $2.41 of list price, and nobody is using it. The "My First Book on..." print titles are on standard colour at a large trim, which KDP prices at $1.00 + $0.0402/page. The same ink at a regular trim within 6.12 x 9 inches is $1.00 + $0.0255/page. On the flagship's 98 pages that is $4.94 against $3.50 of print cost, and it drops the break-even list price from $16.64 to $14.23.

The shelf will pay more than we are asking. The Solar System top ten has a median price of $11.96 and a 90th percentile of $23.33, with slots held at $15.65, $17.70, $19.75 and $23.33 on as few as 4 to 82 ratings. The flagship sits at $11.89 with 16 ratings. It is priced below what its own shelf pays.

That does not generalise. For the 26 forty-page premium-colour picture books that make up the bulk of the catalogue, the price that would clear the royalty bar is $13.43, which is above the 90th percentile of every shelf they sell on. For those books the price fix is not available, and print cost is the reason: premium colour costs $3.60 to print at 40 pages, against $2.30 for a black-ink interior.

8. What died under challenge

Every strong claim went to a reviewer whose job was to refute it. Most were. These are the ones worth knowing about, because they are the conclusions a reasonable person would otherwise reach from this data.

  • "The small high-royalty books are the profitable ones." Selecting books because they had converted and then comparing their cost per order to the flagship's made them look strongly profitable. That is selection on the outcome; it guarantees the result. The unbiased split reverses it.
  • "US click-through collapsed 3.8x." True arithmetic, invalid design: the "before" window excised Jul 27-31, which already read 0.394%. It is a mix effect from auto targeting arriving, not a deterioration.
  • "The foreign zero is decided per market." The aggregate is decided. No individual country is: the UK is the closest and still only p=0.09.
  • "The Aug 9 description republish backfired on six books." A measurement artefact, and a fixable one - see below. All six verdicts are now gone.
  • "Eleven distinct ASINs have converted." Nine.
  • "The account is one book." Not statistically established. The flagship's share of orders is not significantly higher than its share of clicks, and its conversion rate is not distinguishable from the rest of the catalogue.
  • "Only a handful of targets convert, so something is concentrating." Under a single common conversion rate applied to the observed per-target click counts, the expected number of distinct converting targets is 21.3. We observe 16. There is no concentration to explain; it is what a thin click distribution looks like.

9. What we still cannot know, and what it would take

  • Whether any individual book converts. Detecting a real difference at 80% power needs about 265 clicks per book. A US book currently gets 0.25 clicks a day. That is a thousand days per book, so per-book verdicts are not available at this spend and never will be - judge by lane and by series instead.
  • Whether any individual foreign market is dead. The UK needs about 56 clicks against the 34 it has: five more days and roughly $7. Everything else is further away.
  • Anything about the foreign shelves. No rating, review, rank or competitor data exists outside the US. This is a collection gap, not a sample-size problem.
  • Whether reviews cause clicks or clicks cause reviews. Reviews accumulate because books sell, so the arrow plausibly runs the other way, and review count is confounded with book age, rank and series.
  • The description republish, permanently. 89 of 92 books were treated on one day with no holdout, so no counterfactual exists. A future republish should hold 20-30 comparable books back.

10. Fixed today, while looking

Four defects surfaced during this analysis and are now repaired.

A part day was being read as a collapse, for the second time. Every marketplace's 2026-08-12 had closed on its own clock, so settled.py correctly called it settled - but when Amazon built the reports only Australia had finished. The warehouse holds 08-12 as 173 impressions and $0.00 across one market, against nine or ten on every other day. Plotted, that is a 98% drop that never happened. report.day_quality now counts reporting markets per day and marks anything below 60% of the trailing median as a part day, rendered greyed rather than red.

The same part day had invented six failures. outcomes.py ended its measurement window at the clock's settled day, so every action taken on the 9th had a day of zeros pulled into its result. That is where the six *early-backfired* verdicts on the description republish came from. The window now ends at the earlier of the clock and the last day the market actually reported, and all six verdicts disappear.

Four marketplaces had no search-term data at all. Canada and Mexico, and by today the UK and Australia, reject the two-dimension query grouping we ask for, with a 400 that names what they will accept. The failure printed and the run carried on, so the hole read as "no search terms in Canada". reports.run now reads the allowed set out of Amazon's own error and re-requests. Verified against the live API: the UK refused the request and accepted the narrowed one.

Units were requested from Amazon and thrown away. product_days.units was NULL in all 4,143 rows. Now loaded, and it answers a question that was open: 25 units on 25 orders, exactly 1.00 copies per order, so royalty per order equals royalty per unit and no multi-copy order is hiding anywhere.

A caution about every number above

Amazon's restatement runs in both directions, and it just ran downward. Between the report built on 2026-08-11 and the one built today, three of August's orders were removed - 08-05 went 1 to 0, 08-06 went 6 to 5, 08-08 went 2 to 1

  • with clicks unchanged. The account's lifetime total went from 28 to 25, a 12%

reduction, most likely refunds netting out.

The repo has said until today that recent order counts are understatements and therefore floors. They are not floors. They are estimates that can move either way, and a day that improves on a re-pull is no more trustworthy than one that gets worse.
